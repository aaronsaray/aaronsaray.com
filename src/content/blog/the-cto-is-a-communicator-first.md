When people picture a CTO, they picture the best programmer in the building. That's the wrong picture. A CTO is not just a technical thinker. A CTO is a communicator, and the job is building the bridge between the tech team and the rest of the C-suite.

<!--more-->

A few years ago I wrote about [what a CTO is and does](/2021/what-is-a-cto/), and I put communication first back then, too. I'd put it more bluntly now.

## Hands Dirty, About 20% of the Time

A CTO should still write code, review pull requests, and sit in on the technical discussions. But that's about 20% of the job, and it's there for specific reasons:

* To stay current. You can't advise on technology you haven't touched in three years.
* To understand developer feedback. When the team says a system is painful, you should know what they mean from your own hands, not from a summary.
* To understand the way things work these days. Tools, workflows, and expectations change, and secondhand knowledge of them is stale knowledge.

That's it. That 20% is a maintenance budget for your judgment. It is not where the value of the role is.

## The Other 80% Is Communication

The rest of the job is talking, listening, and translating.

It's guidance: helping the team choose a direction without choosing it for them.

It's removing roadblocks: the approvals, the budget, the vendor, the other department that hasn't answered in two weeks.

It's prioritizing: deciding what the team works on, and what it does not.

And then it's taking all of that and making it actually make sense to the stakeholders. The rest of the C-suite doesn't need the technical detail. They need to know what it means for the business, in their language, in time to act on it.

## The Most Important Part: Consequence and Choice

Here's the piece that gets missed. Every priority is a choice, and every choice has a consequence. You can't have it all. If you pick this, you risk that. Ship the feature this quarter and you carry the shortcut for a year. Rebuild the platform and the roadmap stalls. Hire fast and you onboard slow.

The CTO's job is to bring those consequences forward, before the decision, not after. The decision makers get to decide. That's their job. But they can only decide well if someone lays out all of the risks the decision requires, plainly, with the trade-offs attached.

That is what the bridge is for. Not to carry technology across to the business, but to carry the choice and its consequence across, so the people who decide can do it with their eyes open.
